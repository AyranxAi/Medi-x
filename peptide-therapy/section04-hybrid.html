One card, eight distances

You were right that it was not really a ring. The front card had its own box and the seven others shared a second, smaller one — so the jump between them was 0.39 and then the seven barely differed at all. That is a big card standing in front of a flat row.

Every card is the same box now, and distance alone decides how big it arrives: 1 · 0.70 · 0.55 · 0.43 · 0.33 — each step a fixed proportion of the one before it, which is the thing the eye reads as depth. A ladder that descends is not the same as one that descends by the same ratio; the first reads as a row of different-sized cards. The neighbours grew on your note — the cascade was 0.62/0.76 and they read small beside the middle. That costs fan room, and the cost lands where it should: the outer pair tuck further behind each other, and those two carry no name anyway.

And the climb is now the cascade’s too, which is what you drew. A real ring’s near cards sit almost level with the front one — the maths is flat near the middle, so the card beside it was lifting three pixels and looked bottom-aligned with it. Rise is taken from how much smaller a card is instead: 0 · 24 · 37 · 48 · 56px. One cascade drives both size and height, so they can never disagree. That is the one place this ring deliberately stops being physical in order to be legible — and the ring line underneath is drawn from the very same numbers rather than tuned to look right, so it cannot drift off the feet on the next change.
